The Seder Plate

On our table tonight, six items—each a symbol, each a story:

Karpas — Parsley. Dipped in salt water, it holds spring and tears in the same bite.
Maror — Bitter herbs (horseradish). The bitterness of slavery, raw and unapologetic.
Charoset — Sweet fruit, nuts, and wine, representing the mortar the Israelites used to build Pharaoh's monuments. The fact that it tastes good is a feature, not a bug.
Beitzah — A hard-boiled egg. Spring. New life. Also: something that toughens when you apply heat to it, like certain people we know.
Zeroah — The shank bone. The Passover sacrifice, which we no longer perform. We point at the bone.
Orange — A modern addition: a symbol of justice and inclusion for all marginalized members of our community. Someone once said placing an orange on the seder plate made as much sense as a woman being a rabbi. That person was wrong on both counts. The orange stays.

This is a Haggadah—which means the telling. There are fifteen steps. We will do all of them. Nobody is leaving early.

Tonight's Cast

This is a participatory seder. Roles are not optional. If you wanted to be an audience member, you should have stayed home.

Your host is playing God. No notes, please.

God / Narrator
Omniscient. Occasionally exasperated. Still genuinely moved by this story after 3,000 years of tellings. Has complicated feelings about the golden calf. Tonight those feelings are tabled. Played by your host.
Moses
The prophet. Famously reluctant. Famously bad at public speaking—this is historically documented. Pulled off the greatest liberation in human history despite himself.
Aaron
Moses's brother and designated spokesperson. Enthusiastic. Occasionally too enthusiastic. The hype man the situation needed, if not always the one it deserved.
Pharaoh
The antagonist. Most powerful man in the world. Constitutionally unable to learn from his mistakes. Excellent taste in monumental architecture. Will be present tonight even when he's not in a scene.
Miriam
Moses's sister. Prophet. The one who watched from the reeds. The one who had a plan and a song for afterward. Historically underrated. Tonight we fix that.
The Israelites
(2–3 people) The people. Freed from centuries of slavery. Already complaining. It's how they cope. We love them for it.

Everyone participates. The Torah is pretty clear on this. You can complain (kvetch) because it's culturally appropriate, but you still have to do it.

① Kadesh — Blessing Over Wine

First Cup 🍷
God: Welcome, everyone, to our Passover Seder. I am your host and, for tonight's purposes, also God.
God: We begin, as Jews have begun for millennia, with wine. Because the Torah commands it. And also because it helps. We drink four cups tonight—one for each of God's four promises of liberation to the Israelite people. I said: I will bring you out. I will deliver you. I will redeem you. I will take you as my people. Four promises. Four cups. There are no optional cups*.
*except if you're not drinking, we do support you.
God: There is, for the record, a rabbinic debate about whether it should be five cups. There's a fifth promise—I will bring you to the land—and some say that deserves a fifth cup. We never resolved it, and it's frankly problematic. You will see a cup poured for Elijah the prophet later tho—the one who is supposed to show up and settle all unresolved questions. He hasn't arrived yet. We pour the cup anyway. More on that when we get there.
🍷 Pour the first cup. Fill it generously. You will need it.
God: We say the blessing over wine.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, בּוֹרֵא פְּרִי הַגָּפֶן
Baruch Atah Adonai, Eloheinu Melech ha-olam, borei p'ri hagafen.
Blessed are You, our God, Ruler of the Universe,
who creates the fruit of the vine.
God: And the Shehecheyanu—the blessing we say for special moments. For firsts. For returning. This prayer is over 2,000 years old and has been said in every language Jews have spoken, in every country they've lived in or been expelled from, at tables this size and at tables of fifty. Tonight we say it here.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, שֶׁהֶחֱיָנוּ וְקִיְּמָנוּ וְהִגִּיעָנוּ לַזְּמַן הַזֶּה
Baruch Atah Adonai, Eloheinu Melech ha-olam, she-hecheyanu v'key'manu v'higiyanu lazman hazeh.
Blessed are You, who has kept us alive, sustained us, and enabled us to reach this moment.
🍷 Drink the first cup.
Next reader ✦ The rabbis argued about what the four cups really mean. Some said they represent the four matriarchs—Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel, and Leah. Some said they're the four stages of liberation: becoming aware of oppression, opposing it, imagining an alternative, and accepting responsibility to act. The tradition never settled on one answer. Maybe that's the point: freedom requires all of it, and you have to drink all four cups to get there.

② Urchatz — Washing Hands (No Blessing)

God: We wash our hands. Without a blessing—deliberately. Why no blessing yet? Because the blessing comes when we're ready. We've been somewhere else today. We've been on our phones, running late, arriving from full and complicated lives. Now we wash our hands, and we get here. That's all this is.
Pass a bowl of water, a small cup, and a towel. Pour three times over each hand. No blessing. Just water. Just arriving.

③ Karpas — Dipping the Greens

God: We dip parsley in salt water. The parsley is spring—new growth, possibility, the world greening after a long winter. The salt water is tears: the tears of the Israelites in Egypt, the tears of people who don't yet know they'll be freed. We hold both at once. We eat them together. It tastes exactly like what it sounds like. Eat it anyway.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, בּוֹרֵא פְּרִי הָאֲדָמָה
Baruch Atah Adonai, Eloheinu Melech ha-olam, borei p'ri ha-adamah.
Blessed are You, who creates the fruit of the earth.
Dip the parsley in salt water. Eat it. Take a moment with the combination.

④ Yachatz — Breaking the Matzah

God: There are three pieces of matzah on our table. We break the middle one in half. The smaller piece goes back on the plate. The larger piece—the Afikomen—is hidden somewhere in this space. The seder cannot end until it's found. This is either a charming tradition or a very low-stakes hostage situation. It's probably both.
Moses: You know what else was broken in half, sent away, and eventually found? Me. I was put in a basket, floated down a river, raised in a palace, discovered my own identity while standing in front of a bush that was on fire but somehow not burning, at the age of forty. The metaphor practically writes itself.
Next reader: Liberation is made by imperfect, broken people. Broken people are the only kind of people who exist. Don't wait until you're whole to get involved in the repair of the world—you'll be waiting a very long time.
Break the middle matzah. Return the smaller half to the plate. Hide the larger half somewhere in the space. Do it dramatically. Everyone must avert their eyes.
Cover your eyes.

⑤ Maggid — Tell the Story

🍷 Pour the second cup now — but don't drink it yet. It sits in front of you through the entire story. You'll drink it at the end of Maggid, after Dayenu. Think of it as the cup the Israelites hadn't earned yet.

Ha Lachma Anya — The Bread of Affliction

God: We hold up the matzah and say together: Ha lachma anya.
All: This is the bread of affliction—
the humble bread our ancestors ate in Egypt.
Let all who are hungry come and eat.
Next reader ✦ We say this before we've eaten—before we've fed ourselves—because the tradition insists that hospitality cannot wait until you are satisfied. You share what you have when others are hungry, not after. This is not a convenient commandment. It asks us to hold the door open before we've even sat down (although no one is sitting down tonight!). And it's the very first thing we say when the telling begins: not the history, not the explanation, not the context. First: open the door.

The Four Questions

God: Now. The youngest person here.
(A pause while everyone performs the silent calculus of who that is. It's Maria.)
God: Yes. You. We now bully you into asking the questions. As a favor to you, we're also volunt-telling your husband to read the Hebrew.
Youngest/Husband:
מַה נִּשְׁתַּנָּה הַלַּיְלָה הַזֶּה מִכָּל הַלֵּילוֹת
Ma nishtana halaila hazeh mikol haleilot?
Why is this night different from all other nights?
שֶׁבְּכָל־הַלֵּילוֹת, אָֽנוּ אוֹכְלִין חָמֵץ וּמַצָּה; הַלַּֽיְלָה הַזֶּה, כֻּלּוֹ מַצָּה.
Sheb'chol haleilot anu ochlin chametz umatzah, halailah hazeh, kuloh matzah.
On all other nights we eat leavened bread or matzah. Tonight, only matzah.
שֶׁבְּכָל־הַלֵּילוֹת, אָֽנוּ אוֹכְלִין שְׁאָר יְרָקוֹת; הַלַּֽיְלָה הַזֶּה, מָרוֹר.
Sheb'chol haleilot anu ochlin sh'ar y'rakot, halailah hazeh, maror.
On all other nights we eat all kinds of vegetables. Tonight, bitter herbs.
שֶׁבְּכָל־הַלֵּילוֹת, אֵין אָֽנוּ מַטְבִּילִין אֲפִֽלּוּ פַּעַם אֶחָת; הַלַּֽיְלָה הַזֶּה, שְׁתֵּי פְעָמִים.
Sheb'chol haleilot ein anu matbilin afilu pa'am echat; halailah hazeh, sh'tei f'amim.
On all other nights we don't dip our food. Tonight we dip twice.
שֶׁבְּכָל־הַלֵּילוֹת, אָֽנוּ אוֹכְלִין בֵּין יוֹשְׁבִין וּבֵין מְסֻבִּין; הַלַּֽיְלָה הַזֶּה, כֻּלָּֽנוּ מְסֻבִּין.
Sheb'chol haleilot anu ochlin bein yoshvin uvein m'subin; halailah hazeh, kulanu m'subin.
On all other nights we eat either sitting or reclining. Tonight, we all recline.*
God: *The reclining is symbolic. Reclining was how free people ate in the ancient world—leisurely, without fear, taking their time. A slave eats quickly, on their feet, ready to move at any moment. Tonight we are not slaves. We linger. But tonight, we also stand. We tell the whole story. The reclining is symbolic.
🎵 Mah Nishtana

The Four Children

God: The Torah describes four types of people who approach this story. We meet all of them every year at every seder. You may recognize some of them in this room tonight.
Next reader: The Wise Child asks: "What are all these laws and customs? What do they mean? Explain everything."
Next reader ✦ Everything. The whole story, the hard parts, the things we're still arguing about. Genuine curiosity is its own kind of sacred. We tell the Wise Child the full truth: we were slaves, it was terrible, God freed us, freedom comes with obligations, and we're still working on all of them.
Next reader: The Wicked Child asks: "What does this mean to you people?"
Next reader: They said "you"—not "us." They stepped back from the story, watching the Exodus from the shore, wondering if it's really worth getting involved.
Next reader ✦ If you had been in Egypt, would you have walked through the sea? Or would you have looked at the waves and decided it wasn't a good time? We don't ask this to embarrass you. We ask because freedom requires us—all of us, together. You are part of this story whether you opted in or not. Drink your wine and get in the water.
Next reader: The Simple Child asks: "What is this?"
Next reader ✦ With a strong hand, God brought us out of Egypt, out of slavery, into freedom. Not because we were perfect. Not because we deserved it more than others. Because slavery is wrong—and a God who insists on justice eventually makes that point, even when it takes ten plagues to do it.
Next reader: And the one who doesn't know how to ask.
Next reader ✦ We begin for them. We open the door. We say: you are here, and that is the beginning. Your presence is its own kind of question, and it counts.

The Exodus — In One Act

God: And now. The story.
— SCENE ONE: Egypt —
God: Thousands of years ago, in Egypt, the Israelites had been enslaved for generations. They built the empire—its monuments, its granaries, its cities. Their labor was the foundation of everything Pharaoh had. And the empire, as empires do, decided that even this was not enough.
Pharaoh: I have been informed that the Hebrew people are... multiplying. This is frankly alarming. I'm going to need someone to handle it.
God: Pharaoh ordered that every newborn Hebrew boy be thrown into the Nile.
Pharaoh: It was a small policy adjustment.
God: It was not a small policy adjustment.
Pharaoh: I needed stability.
God: You were the most powerful man in the world. You needed nothing.
— SCENE TWO: The Basket —
God: One woman—Yocheved—refused. She waterproofed a basket, put her newborn son inside it, and sent it down the Nile.
Miriam: I was there. I watched from the reeds. I didn't know what was going to happen. I just waited. My mother said: sometimes the only move is to put the thing you love most in the water and trust the current.
God: Pharaoh's daughter found the baby. She knew immediately what he was. She named him Moses. And she raised him as her own, in the palace.
Pharaoh: In my house.
God: In your house.
Pharaoh: (long pause) I did not foresee this.
— SCENE THREE: The Burning Bush (Moses's Least Favorite Story To Tell) —
God: Moses grew up. He saw an Egyptian overseer beating an Israelite slave. He intervened. He had to flee Egypt. He ended up in Midian, married a woman named Tzipporah, became a shepherd, and by all accounts was finally, gratefully, settling into a quiet life with no further obligations.
Moses: I had goats. I was genuinely good with goats.
God: And then I spoke to him from a burning bush.
Moses: For the record: the bush was on fire but was not being consumed, which is unusual, and I went to investigate because I am a curious person. Not because I was looking for a divine mission.
God: Moses. I said: I am the God of your fathers. I have seen the suffering of my people in Egypt. I have heard their cry. Go. Free them.
Moses: And I said: "Who am I, to do this?"
God: I said I would be with him.
Moses: I said: "But what if they don't believe me? What if they don't listen?"
God: I gave him signs. A staff that became a serpent. A hand that turned leprous and then healed. Water from the Nile that became blood.
Moses: I said: "I have never been a good speaker. I'm slow of speech, slow of tongue—please, O Lord, send someone else."
God: (sighing) I gave him Aaron.
Aaron: (waving very enthusiastically) HI. I am ready. I have been ready. This is going to go great.
Moses: I said: "Please. Send someone else."
God: He didn't stop saying that for forty years.
Moses: I was right to be nervous. I want the record to show that I was right to be nervous.
— SCENE FOUR: Pharaoh's Court, Negotiations That Did Not Go Well —
God: Moses and Aaron went to Pharaoh. Ten times. Each time with the same request.
Aaron: We said: "God says—let my people go."
Pharaoh: No.
Aaron: ...God says let my people go.
Pharaoh: Still no.
Moses: I told you he'd say no.
God: I told you he'd say no. I said his heart would be hardened, and you said—
Moses: I said that seemed counterproductive from a negotiation standpoint, yes.
God: And I said: the full display of power is important for the historical record.
Moses: (still not convinced) It seemed like a lot.
God: It was important for the historical record.
Pharaoh: I'm still in the room.

The Ten Plagues

God: I sent plagues. Ten of them. As we name each one, we dip a finger in our wine and let a drop fall onto a plate. Our cup of joy cannot be completely full when we know others suffered to bring about our freedom. Even Pharaoh's people,who had little say in any of this, were human beings. We don't celebrate their pain.
For each plague: dip one finger into your wine and drop it onto a plate or napkin. God will say each plague in Hebrew, and we'll read the English aloud together. After each plague, Pharaoh speaks.
🩸 Blood / Dam / דָּם
Pharaoh: Disgusting. Fine—you can go. ...Wait, no, my magicians did the same trick. Doesn't count.
🐸 Frogs / Tzfardeiya / צְפַרְדֵּעַ
Pharaoh: The frogs I cannot tolerate. You can go. Take them with you. ...The frogs stopped on their own? Never mind.
🦟 Lice / Kinim / כִּנִּים
Pharaoh: My magicians couldn't replicate this one. They said it's the finger of God. I said: still no.
🦁 Wild Beasts / Arov / עָרוֹב
Pharaoh: Fine! Go worship your God! But not too far. ...This just in, the beasts are gone? Actually, I've reconsidered.
🐄 Cattle Disease / Dever / דֶּבֶר
Pharaoh: No.
🔴 Boils / Sh'chin / שְׁחִין
Pharaoh: I am in considerable physical pain. Also: no.
⛈ Hail / Barad / בָּרָד
Pharaoh: God is right. I am wrong. This must stop. Please make it stop. They can go. ...It stopped. You know what? Never mind. They're staying.
🦗 Locusts / Arbeh / אַרְבֶּה
Pharaoh: I have sinned. Please, forgive me, take the locusts and let them go. ...Wind carried the locusts into the sea. Hm. Well. Still no.
🌑 Darkness / Choshech / חֹשֶׁךְ
Pharaoh: Three days. Three days. Fine. Take your children. Leave the livestock.
Moses: Actually, we need the livestock.
Pharaoh:What? Why? That doesn't make any— Get out of my palace.
💀 Death of the Firstborn / Makat B'chorot / מַכַּת בְּכוֹרוֹת
Pharaoh: [quietly] Go.
A Moment ✦ We paused for the ancient plagues—ten drops, ten losses of joy. Now we name the plagues of our own time. Go around: each person names one thing in the world today that diminishes our joy and demands our attention. After each one, dip a finger together.

Climate change. Displacement. Hunger. Indifference to suffering. Injustice. The erosion of truth. Loneliness. The thing you've been thinking about all week. Say it aloud.

The Crossing

Miriam: We left so fast the bread didn't have time to rise. That's why we eat matzah.
God: And then Pharaoh changed his mind (again).
Pharaoh: (from somewhere across the room) WAIT—COME BACK—I CHANGED MY MIND—
God: His army followed them to the Sea of Reeds. There was water ahead and soldiers behind, and no obvious way forward.
Israelites running towards the sea. Pharaoh's army chasing after them.
Israelite 1: This is extremely bad.
Israelite 2: I said something was going to go wrong.
Miriam: You did not say that.
Israelite 3: Were there not enough graves in Egypt? Did we have to come all the way out here to die by the sea?
Moses: Everyone, please— [He raises his arm.]
Israelite 1: Nothing is happening.
Miriam: Wait for it.
God: The waters parted.
(A dramatic pause)
Israelite 3: ...Okay. That's genuinely impressive.
The Israelites cross. Miriam picks up whatever is nearby—a wooden spoon, a phone, a cup—and starts to sing. She always had the right song for the moment.
🎵 Miriam's Song
God: The waters closed. The army did not make it across. The Israelites stood on the far shore and looked at each other.
God: They were free.

Dayenu — It Would Have Been Enough

God: We sing Dayenu:"it would have been enough." It moves through every miracle of the Exodus and says: even just this one thing would have been enough. If God had only brought us out of Egypt:Dayenu. If God had only parted the sea: Dayenu. If God had only given us the Torah: Dayenu.
Next reader: But Dayenu is also an opportunity to reflect. If each thing would have been enough, and then there was more... the song asks us to notice that we have always already had more than enough. And that gratitude doesn't mean complacency. We are grateful, and we know there is still more work to do. Both are true at once.
A Moment ✦ Go around the room. Each person names one thing that would already be enough in their life and after each one, we all respond:

⑥ Rachtzah — Washing Hands Again

God: We wash our hands again. This time with a blessing, because now we're about to eat. The first washing was about arriving. This one is about intention. It says: I know what I'm doing. I'm ready. I'm here on purpose.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו וְצִוָּנוּ עַל נְטִילַת יָדַיִם
Baruch Atah Adonai, Eloheinu Melech ha-olam, asher kidshanu b'mitzvotav v'tzivanu al netilat yadayim.
Blessed are You, who commands us regarding the washing of hands.
Pass the bowl again. Pour three times over each hand. Dry them. This time with intention.

⑦–⑧ Motzi Matzah — Eat the Matzah

God: Matzah. The bread of affliction. The bread of haste. The bread of people who were so ready to leave that they could not wait for it to rise. It is not a great bread. And yet we eat it every year because the point is not the bread. The point is the leaving. The point is to remember that not all are yet free.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם הַמּוֹצִיא לֶחֶם מִן הָאָרֶץ
Baruch Atah Adonai, Eloheinu Melech ha-olam, ha-motzi lechem min ha-aretz.
Blessed are You, who brings forth bread from the earth.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו וְצִוָּנוּ עַל אֲכִילַת מַצָּה
Baruch Atah Adonai, Eloheinu Melech ha-olam, asher kidshanu b'mitzvotav v'tzivanu al achilat matzah.
Blessed are You, who commands us to eat matzah.
Pass around the matzah. Each person takes a piece and eats it.(Sorry)

⑨ Maror — The Bitter Herbs

God: We eat bitter herbs. Horseradish that is raw, strong, unapologetic. The bitterness of slavery. The tradition is to eat it without softening it, so that we feel, briefly and mildly, something of what bitterness means.
Next reader ✦ The bitterness we taste tonight isn't only historical. Slavery has not ended. it has changed its name and become harder to see. When you go to the grocery store: who picked the produce? Who sewed the clothes you're wearing? Who harvested the coffee beans? Modern supply chains run, in part, on coerced and exploited labor that we are designed not to think about. The maror asks us to think about it.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו וְצִוָּנוּ עַל אֲכִילַת מָרוֹר
Baruch Atah Adonai, Eloheinu Melech ha-olam, asher kidshanu b'mitzvotav v'tzivanu al achilat maror.
Blessed are You, who commands us to eat bitter herbs.
Take a piece of horseradish. Eat.

⑩ Korech — The Hillel Sandwich

God: Now we make a sandwich. This comes from Hillel, a great rabbi, who used to eat his matzah and maror together in one bite. He said they belong together. Bitterness and sweetness and liberation: not separate, not sequential, but simultaneous. That's what Passover keeps insisting on. You don't get the freedom without acknowledging the bitterness that came before it. You eat them together, or you're not eating the whole meal.
Eat Hillel's sandwich: matzah + horseradish + charoset.

Before We Eat — Cups Three and Four

God: In this family, we do all four cups before the meal. Which means: two more now, plus Elijah and Miriam, and then we eat. This is not the standard arrangement, but it's a party.

Elijah's Cup — and Miriam's

God: Remember that fifth cup I mentioned at the very beginning? This is it. We fill a cup for Elijah the prophet,the one who is supposed to arrive and settle all unresolved debates, including whether there should be a fifth cup. He hasn't arrived. We pour it anyway. On Passover night, the tradition says he travels to every seder in the world (like Santa Claus). We open the door for him.
Fill Elijah's cup. Open the front door. Stand at the threshold. Look out into the night..
God: Did Elijah drink from his cup? [Examine it carefully. There will be no consensus.] The tradition says he takes a sip at every seder. The tradition also says we don't know when he'll arrive. These two things are in tension and we sit with that tension. He hasn't arrived yet because the work isn't finished. Every year we open the door because hope requires an open door.
Miriam: While we wait for Elijah—there is also Miriam's Cup. This one is filled with water. Legend says that a well traveled with the Israelites through all forty years of desert wandering. It was called Miriam's Well. Water and wine. Elijah and Miriam. The prophet who is coming, and the prophet who was always already there.
Each person pours a little from their water glass into Miriam's Cup. Close the door.

⑫ Tzafun — Finding the Afikomen

God: The Afikomen (the piece of matzah we broke and hid at the very start of the night) must now be found. We have drunk all four cups. We have eaten the meal. But the seder cannot formally end until the Afikomen is located and ransomed back. The final taste in our mouths must be matzah. The bread of liberation gets the last word.
God: Someone in this room knows where it is. Or has already found it and is waiting to negotiate. Both are legitimate strategies.
Search for the Afikomen. The finder negotiates ransom before handing it over. No demand is too unreasonable. Suggestions: the host sings a song, tells an embarrassing story, makes a promise, owes a favor. Once ransomed, break it into pieces. Everyone eats a piece.
